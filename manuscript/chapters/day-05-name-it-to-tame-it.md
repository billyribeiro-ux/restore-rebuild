# Name It to Tame It

## The Word

> Trust in him at all times, you people. Pour out your heart before him. God is a refuge for us.
>
> — Psalm 62:8

## Where You Are Today

Someone asked you yesterday how you were doing.

And you heard yourself say it — *yeah, not my best month, bit of a rough patch* — in a voice so light and so practised that even you almost believed it. You watched their face relax. You changed the subject smoothly, because you are good at this, and then you carried the whole undischarged weight of it into the next room and the next hour and into bed with you.

There is a specific loneliness in being asked and answering falsely. It is worse than not being asked. And the strangest part is that you did not do it to protect them. You did it because putting the real thing into words felt dangerous — as though naming it plainly would make it larger, or make it real in a way it is not yet.

The opposite is true, and it is one of the better-supported findings in the whole of affective neuroscience.

## What Is Happening In Your Brain

Putting a feeling into words reduces the intensity of the feeling. Researchers call it **affect labelling**, and you can watch it happen inside the skull.

In a well-known set of studies led by Matthew Lieberman, people were shown images of distressed faces while their brains were scanned. When they simply looked, the amygdala — the threat-detection structure we met on Day 1 — responded strongly. When they were asked to choose a *word* for the emotion they were seeing, something specific happened: **amygdala activity went down**, and activity rose in a region of the prefrontal cortex associated with verbal processing and inhibition. The naming was doing the damping.

Notice what this is not. It is not venting. It is not the intensity of the expression that helps — shouting about it produces no such effect, and Day 8 will show you that unstructured emotional discharge is one of the few things in this literature that reliably fails. What helps is the **precision of the label**. Vague words do vague work. There is good evidence that people who habitually distinguish between fine-grained emotional states — who can tell *ashamed* from *humiliated* from *disappointed* — regulate themselves better under stress and drink less to cope.

Which gives you a concrete problem to solve. Right now you have one word for what you feel, and the word is *bad*. Under that word are at least four separate things, and they need different responses. Fear about the future is not the same as shame about being seen. Grief for the money is not the same as grief for the self-image. Anger at yourself is not the same as anger at the market. You have been carrying them in a single unlabelled sack, and a single unlabelled sack cannot be put down, because you cannot put down a thing you have not named.

There is one more finding worth having. The effect works even when people do not believe it is working, and even when it feels *worse* in the moment. Naming often stings on the way in. That sting is not a sign you have made a mistake. It is the sound of a vague dread becoming a specific, and therefore finite, thing.

## What the Word Says To It

*"Pour out your heart before him."*

The Hebrew image is of emptying a vessel — tipping it up and letting the contents run out. Not summarising the contents. Not editing them into something more presentable first. Pouring.

The Psalms are the least polite book in the Bible, and that is precisely their genius. Their authors accuse God of forgetting them, of sleeping, of hiding. They describe their bones wasting and their eyes failing. Psalm 88 ends in unrelieved darkness with no resolution at all — a poem of despair that the people of God have kept in their songbook for millennia rather than tidying it away. Whoever assembled this collection believed that God can be told the truth, including the ugly parts, including the parts that sound like blasphemy when written down.

So the invitation is not *tell God the acceptable version*. Nobody in Scripture does that. And notice what the verse offers in exchange: not a solution, but a **refuge**. A place to be while it is still bad.

You gave a false answer yesterday because you had nowhere safe to give a true one. Here is somewhere.

## The Meditation

Five minutes today. Sit down where you will not be interrupted.

Breathe in for four, out for six, six times.

Now do the naming. Slowly, aloud if you can, complete this sentence four separate times, with a different word each time:

*"What I actually feel is ______."*

Push past the first word. The first word is usually *bad* or *stressed* and it is almost always a lid on something more specific. Keep going until you find one that makes your chest tighten slightly when you say it. That tightening is how you know you have located the real one.

Then take the sharpest of the four and say it to God, plainly, in your own words. Not a prayer with the grammar of a prayer. Just the thing itself: *I am ashamed. I cannot look at myself.*

Then be quiet for thirty seconds. Do not fill the silence.

Close: *"God is a refuge for us."* One long exhale.

## Say It Out Loud

1. I can name what I feel, and naming it makes it smaller, not larger.
2. I do not have to edit myself before I bring myself to God.
3. What I am carrying has a name, a size, and an end.

## Today's Small Thing

Go back to whoever asked you yesterday — or find one person who is safe — and correct the record.

One sentence. *When you asked how I was, I gave you the easy answer. The truth is I am struggling.* You do not have to explain further, and if they ask, you may say you would rather not go into it yet.

You are not asking them to fix anything. You are practising the difference between a private life and a false one.

## Carry This

The thing I refuse to name is the thing that grows in the dark; the thing I name out loud is the thing I can finally see the edges of.
