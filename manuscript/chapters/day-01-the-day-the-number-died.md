# The Day the Number Died

## The Word

> Yahweh is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves those who have a crushed spirit.
>
> — Psalm 34:18

## Where You Are Today

You keep checking it.

You know the number. You have known it since it happened. And still, six, eight, twelve times a day, your thumb finds the app and you look — as though the figure might have quietly repaired itself while you were making lunch. It never has. You look anyway, and every time you look, something in your chest drops through the floor again, and every time it drops you feel slightly more ashamed of yourself for having checked.

There is a taste to this. A metal taste at the back of the throat. Food is not interesting. Sleep arrives late and leaves early. You are functioning — answering messages, saying the right things — and underneath the functioning there is a low continuous hum of dread, like a fridge in an empty house.

Today we are not going to fix any of that. Today we are only going to name what it is.

## What Is Happening In Your Brain

What you are feeling is not an overreaction. It is your brain doing exactly what it was built to do, with more force than you were ready for.

Two findings explain most of today.

The first: **losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.** Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky demonstrated this in the 1970s, and it has held up across half a century and thousands of studies. Winning ten thousand is pleasant. Losing ten thousand is *devastating* — not twice as unpleasant in some abstract accounting sense, but twice as heavy in the body. This asymmetry is not a flaw you developed. It is standard human equipment, and it is old. An ancestor who shrugged off losses did not keep a food store through winter.

There is a striking piece of evidence for how deep this runs. Researchers studied people with damage to a small almond-shaped structure called the **amygdala** — the brain's threat alarm. Those individuals showed almost no loss aversion at all. They gambled on losses without flinching. Which tells us something important: the anguish you are feeling is not generated by your reasoning. It is generated by an alarm system that sits underneath your reasoning and does not take instruction from it. You cannot logic your way out of this, and you should stop trying today. That is not what it is for.

The second finding: **your brain processes this kind of loss on the same machinery it uses for physical pain.** Studies of social rejection found that being excluded lit up a region called the anterior cingulate cortex — the same region that responds to bodily injury. This is why the language everyone reaches for is physical. Gutted. Winded. A blow. Sick. You are not being dramatic when you say it hurts. Some of the circuitry involved is literally the circuitry of hurt.

So: your chest is tight because an ancient alarm is sounding, and it aches because your brain has filed this under *injury*. Both systems are working correctly. Neither is under your command this morning.

This is why the first instruction of this book is not *think differently*. It is *be treated*. You do not argue with a wound. You clean it and you give it time.

## What the Word Says To It

Look closely at what David actually writes.

He does not say God is near to those who are coping. He does not say God draws near once you have made a plan, taken responsibility, and demonstrated the right attitude about your circumstances. He says God is near to the **broken-hearted**, and that He saves those with a **crushed spirit**.

Crushed. It is the word for something pressed flat under weight. Not bruised — crushed.

There is no requirement here that you pull yourself together first. The nearness is not a reward for recovery; it is offered to the wreckage, on the morning of the wreckage, while the wreckage is still smoking. Whatever else you are uncertain of today, you are qualified for this verse. You meet its only condition.

And notice what it does *not* promise. It does not promise the money back. It promises presence. Today that has to be enough, and — if you will let it be — it is.

## The Meditation

Four minutes. Sit down, feet flat, hands open on your knees, palms up. Open hands, not fists; your body reports its posture back to your brain, and today we are telling it we are not fighting.

Close your eyes.

Breathe in through your nose for a count of four. Hold for two. Out through your mouth, slowly, for a count of six. The long exhale is the part that matters — it is the signal your body reads as *the danger has passed*.

Do that six times without hurrying.

Now let one line rest in your mind. Do not analyse it. Just let it sit there the way you would let sun sit on your face:

*"Near to the broken-hearted."*

When your mind runs to the number — and it will, within seconds — do not scold yourself. Notice it, and come back to the line. *Near to the broken-hearted.* You may have to come back twenty times in four minutes. Twenty returns is not twenty failures. Twenty returns is the exercise.

Finish by breathing out once, longer than feels natural, and opening your eyes.

## Say It Out Loud

Say these where you can hear your own voice. Once each, slowly.

1. My pain is real, and it is not proof that I am weak.
2. I am not required to be whole today to be held today.
3. God is near to me in the wreckage, and I do not have to earn His nearness.

## Today's Small Thing

Delete the trading app from your phone's home screen. Not from the phone — just from the front page, so that looking requires a deliberate search rather than a thumb reflex.

Then, on paper, write the number. The actual figure. Write it once, plainly, without commentary. Close the notebook.

You have looked at it on purpose today. That is different from being ambushed by it, and it is the beginning of getting the number back under your authority instead of the other way round.

## Carry This

The wound is real, and I am not being asked to pretend otherwise — only to stop checking whether it has healed by pressing on it.
