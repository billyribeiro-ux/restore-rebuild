# Letting Go Is a Decision, Not a Feeling

## The Word

> Brothers, I don't regard myself as yet having taken hold, but one thing I do: forgetting the things which are behind and stretching forward to the things which are ahead, I press on toward the goal.
>
> — Philippians 3:13–14

## Where You Are Today

You wrote it down yesterday, and something in you expected the writing to work like a discharge — that you would close the notebook and feel the pressure drop.

It did not drop. If anything you slept worse. And so you are back where you have been since about Day 3, waiting for a particular internal event to occur: the moment when it stops hurting enough that you can put it down. You are treating letting go as a **feeling that will eventually arrive**, and you are waiting for its arrival before you allow yourself to move.

It is not going to arrive in that order. That is not how the mechanism runs, and today is about turning the sequence the right way round.

## What Is Happening In Your Brain

Start with what does not work, because you have been doing it all week.

In a now-famous experiment, Daniel Wegner asked people not to think about a white bear. They were to ring a bell each time it came to mind. Not only did the bear come constantly, but afterwards — when the instruction was lifted — the suppression group thought about it *more* than people who had never been told to avoid it. Suppression produces rebound. The very act of monitoring for a thought keeps the thought active, because the monitor has to hold the target in mind in order to check for it.

Which explains something you have noticed. Every morning you resolve not to dwell on it. Every morning the resolution is the first thing that puts it in front of you.

Now the alternative, which comes out of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy — a body of work built by Steven Hayes and tested across hundreds of trials. Its central construct is **psychological flexibility**: the ability to have an unpleasant internal experience *while continuing to act in the direction you care about*. Its opposite, **experiential avoidance**, is the habit of arranging your behaviour around not feeling bad — and experiential avoidance is one of the more reliable predictors of poor outcomes across a wide range of conditions.

The move ACT teaches is not to feel better before acting. It is to **act while feeling badly** — deliberately, in a chosen direction, with the discomfort fully present and unresolved.

So "letting go" is not the moment the grip releases. It is a decision you make, repeatedly, while the grip is still tight: the decision to stop organising your day around the loss. The feeling follows the behaviour, often by weeks. Anyone who has waited for the feeling first has waited a very long time.

There is a practical translation of this for you, and you will use it for the rest of your career. **You do not need to feel confident in order to follow your process.** That sentence is the whole of Day 21 in advance. The trader who requires a particular internal state before executing has made his edge conditional on his mood, and his mood is the least reliable instrument on the desk.

## What the Word Says To It

Paul writes *forgetting the things which are behind* — and he is a man with a great deal behind him, some of it genuinely terrible. He had persecuted people. He had held the coats at a stoning. If anyone had material for a three-in-the-morning tape, it was him.

But look at the grammar. **"One thing I do."** Not one thing I feel. Not one thing I have finally been released from. A verb of action, in the present continuous, from a man who begins the sentence by admitting *I don't regard myself as yet having taken hold* — that is, he explicitly denies having arrived.

And the image he chooses is athletic: *stretching forward*, the word for a runner leaning into the tape, whole body committed to a direction. That is not a description of an inner state. It is a description of a posture chosen and held.

Notice too that forgetting, here, is not amnesia. Paul rehearses his own past in his letters when it serves. What he refuses is the past as a *place to live*. He has decided which direction his weight is going, and he redecides it, evidently, on days when he does not feel like it — which is why he keeps writing about it.

The permission in this verse is large: you may press on without having taken hold. You may run before the grief is finished. You are not required to be free in order to move.

## The Meditation

Four minutes. Sit. Feet flat.

Breathe in for four, out for six, six times.

Now, deliberately, invite the thing in. Let the loss be present — the number, the memory, the fear. Do not push it away and do not engage with it. Picture it as an object placed beside you on the floor. Heavy, real, not going anywhere today.

Say inwardly: *"You can stay. I am not fighting you today."*

Then, with it still there, say the second half:

*"And I am going forward anyway."*

Sit for a full minute with both of those true at once — the weight beside you, and your intention pointed elsewhere. That coexistence is the skill. Not the removal of the weight. The coexistence.

Close with: *"One thing I do."* One long exhale.

## Say It Out Loud

1. I do not have to feel free in order to move forward, and I am moving forward today.
2. I can carry this and still do the next right thing.
3. I press on before I have taken hold, because that is what pressing on means.

## Today's Small Thing

Do one thing you have been postponing until you "feel better".

Not a trading task. A life task — the gym session you have skipped for two weeks, the call to your mother, the drawer of paperwork, the run you used to do on Thursdays. Choose something small and physical and unrelated to markets.

Do it badly. Do it while feeling awful. Then note, in the notebook, one line: *Did it. Still felt bad. Did it anyway.*

That line is your first piece of evidence in a file we are going to build all month: proof that your actions are no longer downstream of your mood.

## Carry This

I have been waiting to feel ready, and readiness has never once been the thing that arrives first.
